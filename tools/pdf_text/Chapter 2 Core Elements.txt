2. Core Elements of μML 
 
The μML is a visual and logical modelling language for designing software systems.  It is based around a 
small number of core elements, known as concepts, connectives, features and regions.  When used by 
human designers, the focus is on developing software diagrams using the μML notations.  When these 
diagrams are mapped onto logical graphs consisting of vertices, edges and labelled properties, the focus 
is on manipulating and refining software models, using automatic algorithms.  This is why μML is suitable 
for Model-Driven Engineering. 
2.1 Visual Design Principles 
As a visual diagram notation, μML consists of concepts and connectives, features and regions.  The 
vertices of the notation are known as concepts, distinguished by the icon or geometric shape used to 
depict the concept.  The connecting edges of the notation are known as connectives, distinguished by the 
kind of line, connector or arrow used to depict the connective.  For the sake of familiarity, many of the 
icons adopted in μML are reused from UML [1, 2] or from Structured Analysis and Design [3, 4].  The 
essence of μML is simplicity, uniformity and consistency: 
• Principle 1:  Use the simplest notation to distinguish each concept and connective. 
• Principle 2:  Use the same notation to depict the same thing across different models. 
• Principle 3:  Try where possible to adopt or adapt notations familiar to engineers. 
• Principle 4:  Notational variations should cue similar adapted meanings across all models. 
• Principle 5:  Connective paths should be combined or simplified where possible. 
Different diagrams offer different perspectives of a software system, emphasising certain aspects, while 
suppressing other aspects.  Some diagrams represent structural models, describing static relationships 
between concepts.  Other diagrams represent behavioural models, describing the dynamic behaviour of 
the system.  Each fundamental kind of diagram has its own distinct notation for its concepts, to avoid 
ambiguity and prevent misinterpretation, but diagrams may share other notations. 
Wherever the same notations are used, they mean the same thing, even across different diagrams.  Some 
examples of this are the generalisation and composition arrows, which express structural relationships.  It 
is also important for visual consistency that similar stylings mean the same kind of thing across different 
diagrams.  An example of this is the way in which solid and dashed lines are used:  the solid line always 
represents the canonical meaning, whereas the dashed line means the adapted, but related meaning. 
The diagrams created using the μML notation are also models, since they each represent some abstract 
model of the depicted software system.  Each model offers only a partial view of a software system.  The 
purpose of Model-Driven Engineering is to combine different models from different perspectives, in a 
process that we call folding the views together
1.  When focusing on the visual aspect, we refer to the 
diagram, but when focusing on the logical aspect, we refer to the model. 
2.2 Common Concepts in μML 
The vertices in μML are referred to as concepts, which is the most general term we could find to describe 
a node of interest.  Concepts can be entity-like, such as Actor, System or Object.  Other concepts can be 
behavioural, such as Task, Process or State.

• Actor – an external human stakeholder acting in a defined role.  An actor is depicted as a stick 
figure with a descriptive label appearing below.  The name describes the role, rather than the 
individual.  See also the Task Model (chapter 3). 
• System – a computer system, or subsystem, acting in a defined role.  It could represent an 
external system that interacts with this system.  It could represent the outline view of a 
subsystem that is described in detail elsewhere.  A system is depicted as a labelled 3D box. 
• Object – a structured type consisting of labelled properties describing attributes, references or 
operations.  An object is depicted as a named rectangle, with optional internal partitions to 
describe its attributes, references or operations.

Object:  denotes an information entity, typically a datatype 
created, updated or deleted by the current system.

2.3 Common Connectives in μML 
The edges in μML are referred to as connectives, which is the most general term we could find to describe 
both static relationships and dynamic transitions.  Structural connectives include the Generalisation, 
Composition and Association relationships.  Dynamic connectives include the Transition and Dataflow 
arrows. 
Figure 2.3 describes some of the common structural connectives in μML.  These include: 
• Generalisation – a directed edge, denoting the “a kind of” relationship, whose source is the 
specific concept and whose target is the general concept.  A generalisation is drawn as an arrow 
with a solid shaft and a triangular arrowhead5. 
• Composition – a directed edge, denoting the “a part of” relationship, whose source is the 
component part and whose target is the composite whole.  A composition is drawn as an arrow 
with a solid shaft and a diamond arrowhead. 
• Association – an undirected edge, denoting the “is related to” relationship connecting two 
concepts.  An association may be adorned with further features adding additional meaning.  An 
association is depicted using a simple line connector. 
 
Generalisation: denotes the "a kind of" relationship between a 
specific and a general concept.  The arrow points to the general 
concept.  
Composition: denotes the "a part of" relationship between a 
part and a whole concept.  The arrow points to the whole 
concept.  
Association:  denotes the "is related to" relationship between 
two different concepts.  This relationship is not directed. 
 
Figure 2.3:  Structural Connectives 
 
Transition:  denotes any kind of control edge linking the source 
to the target.  The arrow points to the target concept. 
 
Dataflow:  denotes any kind of flow transferring data from the 
source to the target.  The arrow points to the target concept. 
 
Figure 2.4:  Dynamic Connectives 
Figure 2.4 describes some of the common dynamic connectives in μML.  These include: 
• Transition – a directed control edge linking the source to the target.  It is used to indicate a state 
transition, or a navigation route, or a reference from the source to the target.  A transition is 
depicted as an arrow with a solid shaft and an open arrowhead.

• Dataflow – a directed flow edge linking the source to the target.  It is used to indicate a dataflow,

2.4 Regions and Features of μML 
The other common elements of μML include things which are neither vertices nor edges, but which are 
present in many models, with similar meanings.  These include elements describing enclosed areas of a 
model, and descriptive elements attached to other model elements. 
The enclosing areas in μML are referred to as regions.  The kinds of region include: 
• Diagram – a region containing all the elements of a particular model.  Any instance of a model is 
contained within a diagram, which is the top-level container for that model. 
• Boundary – a partitioning region enclosing certain elements of a model but excluding other 
elements.  A boundary can denote the scope of a system, a version or release of a system, or the 
expansion of a state or process, to show nested elements within that boundary. 
The other descriptive elements in μML are referred to as features, since this is the most general term we 
could think of.  These may qualify other elements as adornments or may denote other kinds of property 
of an element.  Some of the kinds of feature include: 
• Attribute – a property of a structured entity, denoting a value having a simple type. 
• Reference – a property of a structured entity, denoting a reference to another entity.  This is 
drawn as a transition from the source to the target. 
• Operation – a behaviour of a structured entity, expressed as a signature with inputs and outputs. 
• Event – a descriptive label attached to a transition, denoting the trigger of behaviour. 
This list is not exhaustive, and individual models may contain further variants. 
2.5 Rules of the μML Notation 
The μML notation has the following syntactic and semantic rules: 
• Type-level concepts – concepts denote type-level entities, not instances.  E.g. an object Loan 
denotes the loan-type, not an instance.  An actor Member denotes the role of a member, not any 
individual (a definition rule). 
• Type-level connectives – connectives denote typical relationships, not instances of these 
relationships.  E.g. an association borrows denotes the typical relationship between a library 
Member and a Book, not a specific instance of this relationship (a definition rule). 
• Type-level features – features denote typical, rather than actual, features.  E.g. an argument 
date denotes the formal argument to a process, not the actual argument (a definition rule). 
• Composition kinds – there are two kinds of composition: structural and material.  The default 
kind is structural (the whole depends on its parts).  The material kind is co-dependent (the parts 
depend on the whole) [5]. 
• Generalisation kinds – there are two kinds of generalisation: disjoint and overlapping.  The 
default kind is disjoint (subtypes do not overlap).  
2.6 Core Metamodel 
The core elements of the μML may be defined in a simple metamodel.  In figure 2.5, we use a metamodel 
written in the ReMoDeL metamodel definition language [6].  ReMoDeL is a toolkit for Model-Driven 
Engineering that supports pure functional object-oriented specifications of metamodels.  It offers the

benefit of reusing elements across different metamodels.  Elements may be refined or redefined in 
successive metamodels.   
 
# The Core Model from muML (Micro Modelling Language).  This is the 
# foundation metamodel, from which other metamodels may be derived. 
# It consists of the core elements:  Element, Concept, Connective, 
# Feature and Region.  Element is the optionally named root concept. 
# Feature describes a named atomic feature.  Concept describes a  
# named vertex.  Connective describes a linking edge.  Region 
# denotes a partition or boundary. 
 
metamodel CoreModel { 
 
# Element is the root of every metamodel.  An Element may  
# optionally have a name. 
 
   concept Element { 
      attribute name : String 
      operation toString : String { name } 
   } 
 
# Concept is a structured kind of Element denoting a named vertex. 
# A structured Concept usually consists of other Features. 
 
   concept Concept inherit Element { 
   } 
    
# Connective is a linking kind of Element denoting an edge.  A 
# Connective will have a source and target Element. 
 
   concept Connective inherit Element { 
   } 
    
# Feature is a simple kind of Element denoting a named and typed 
# feature. 
 
   concept Feature inherit Element { 
   } 
 
# Region is an enclosing kind of Element denoting a boundary. 
# A region will include or exclude other elements. 
 
   concept Region inherit Element { 
   } 
 
} 
Figure 2.5:  Metamodel of the core concepts in the ReMoDeL language. 
In the metamodel shown in figure 2.5, only the most basic sketch is given for Element, Concept, 
Connective, Feature and Region.  This is because in derived metamodels, the related meta-types are 
specialised to the needs of that metamodel.  Nonetheless, we specify that: 
• Element – may optionally be named; 
• Concept – is a kind of Element; 
• Connective – is a kind of Element; 
• Feature – is a kind of Element; 
• Region – is a kind of Element. 
The reason for not specifying more detail than this is because the types of related elements are not 
declared until the derived metamodels.

2.7 Differences from UML 
In terms of differences between μML and other notations, of which the most prominent is UML [1, 2], we 
have made a number of simplifications.  We have revised the icons used for concepts across different 
diagrams, so that the same concept is represented everywhere by the same icon.  In particular: 
• We have separated out the visual notation for Process and State. 
• We have separated out the visual notation for Actor and System.  
These separations of concern bring semantic clarity in models.  The other μML nodes are similar to UML 
in their depiction and meaning. 
Another difference between μML and UML is in how all of the concepts Task, Object, Process and State 
can be decomposed in the same sense via the Composition relationship: 
• A composite Task can be decomposed into component Tasks. 
• A composite Object can be decomposed into component Objects. 
• A composite Process can be decomposed into component Processes. 
• A composite State can be decomposed into component States. 
Decomposition of this kind is supported for Classes and Activities in UML, but not in the same way for 
Use-Cases and States.  Use-Cases participate in include and extend relations, but these have no reliable 
structural interpretation [7].  States can have embedded state machines in UML, but this is not based on 
a theory of state refinement [8]. 
We have also simplified the connectives used to join concepts, so that broadly the same meaning is 
represented everywhere by the same connective.  In particular: 
• We have reserved Association for conceptual, unresolved relationships. 
• We have reused Composition for every kind of composition or aggregation. 
• We have reserved Transition for every kind of directed navigable relationship. 
• We have reserved Dataflow for every kind of procedural or invocation transfer. 
Apart from reducing the overall number of connectives in the notation, we have applied the same 
connective in more circumstances.  In particular, the Composition relationship is used with Tasks.  When 
used with Processes, this has the flavour of a structured program [9].  The reservation of Association for 
undirected conceptual relationships and Transition for directed concrete relationships simplifies the 
construction of a meta-model for the purposes of Model-Driven Engineering.
